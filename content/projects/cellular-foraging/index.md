A swimming cell is a noisy measurement.
Tracking enough of them, for long enough, to learn the rule they use is slow.
A photograph of the colony has already averaged over many cells.

With Naama Brenner and Ahmed El Hady I map simple foraging rules onto quantities you can read from that photograph: where the center of the colony sits, which way the cells point, how much wider the colony is than diffusion alone would make it, and how its shape is stretched.
The rules are the elementary ones.
A cell can tumble less often when it is climbing a nutrient gradient, or it can simply swim more slowly where food is rich.
Each rule turns the colony-scale signals on at its own power of time, so a time series can tell them apart.

In a patchy nutrient landscape the practical consequence is a separation of clocks.
The colony can finish harvesting a local patch long before its density has settled across the whole dish.
The extra width and the shape tensor are what separate "the colony is drifting" from "cells are spending more time where the food is."
Individual tracks stay fluctuation-dominated.
The spatial average is what makes a standard colony image diagnostic.

This is a manuscript in preparation: [Physics of cellular foraging](/papers/rojas-foraging/).
